Most of the confusion I hear from internationally trained doctors comes down to two sections of one Act. They sound alike, they are often mentioned in the same breath, and they do different jobs. Once you separate them, most of the "can I bill here?" questions answer themselves.

## The short version

- **Section 19AA** is about *what* you can bill. It asks whether you hold specialist registration, or are on an approved training or workforce programme. It applies to every doctor first registered in Australia on or after 1 November 1996, and there is no exemption from it. [VERIFY: confirm the 1 November 1996 date and the "no exemption" wording on the 19AA page]
- **Section 19AB** is about *where* you can bill. It restricts overseas-trained doctors, and foreign graduates of Australian or New Zealand medical schools, to certain locations for at least ten years from the date of their first Australian medical registration. This is the 10-year moratorium. [VERIFY: confirm "from the time you get your medical registration in Australia" is the trigger, not the date of arrival or first job]

You can be clear of one and still caught by the other. That is the single most common misunderstanding, and it is the subject of the next article, on whether fellowship ends the moratorium.

## Section 19AA: what you can bill

To provide services that attract a Medicare rebate to private patients, a doctor who is an Australian citizen or permanent resident must hold specialist registration with the Medical Board of Australia, or be enrolled in an approved training programme under section 3GA of the Act and working towards specialist registration. [VERIFY: current list of approved 3GA programmes]

For a general practitioner, specialist registration in general practice comes with fellowship of the RACGP or ACRRM. [VERIFY: confirm that FRACGP and FACRRM lead to specialist registration in the specialty of general practice under the National Law]

If you are a temporary resident, section 19AA does not require you to hold specialist registration. You can bill under the general rebate schedule while restricted by 19AB. Specialist registration still matters, because it gives access to the higher rebates for your specialty. [VERIFY: paraphrase of the temporary residents section of the 19AA page]

One trap worth naming. If you become a permanent resident before you gain specialist registration, and you are not on an approved 3GA programme, you lose access to Medicare benefits for private patients until one of those two things is true. The Department's page says to tell Services Australia of any residency change as soon as it happens, to avoid a Medicare debt. [VERIFY: wording of the "gaining permanent residency before specialist registration" section]

## Section 19AB: where you can bill

If you obtained your primary medical degree outside Australia or New Zealand, or you studied medicine in Australia or New Zealand as a temporary resident, section 19AB applies to you.

For at least ten years from your first Australian medical registration you must work in a location that qualifies. For a GP that means a Distribution Priority Area (DPA). For a non-GP specialist it means a District of Workforce Shortage (DWS). [VERIFY: DPA for GPs and DWS for non-GP specialists, per the 19AB page]

Three practical points follow from that.

1. **The restriction runs from registration, not from your first job.** Time spent registered but not working in a DPA still counts towards the ten years. [VERIFY: whether any conditions apply to time not spent in an eligible location]
2. **A salaried public hospital position that does not attract Medicare benefits is not restricted.** The moratorium is about Medicare provider numbers, not about where you may practise medicine at all. [VERIFY: "no restriction if you work in a salaried position that doesn't attract Medicare benefits"]
3. **Exemptions are tied to a location.** Working in a DPA location is itself the main route to an exemption. Exemptions to work outside a DPA are limited and specific: a six-month locum exemption, a replacement exemption, a spousal exemption, after-hours work, prior employment negotiations, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander primary health services, and a discretionary category. Each has its own evidence requirements. [VERIFY: current list of exemption types on the exemptions page]

## Where DPA comes in

DPA is a classification the Department applies to GP catchments, not to suburbs or postcodes. It is reviewed annually. Areas classified MM 2 to MM 7 under the Modified Monash Model are automatically DPA. Inner metropolitan MM 1 areas are not. Other MM 1 catchments are assessed against a benchmark of GP services each year, which is why some outer-metropolitan catchments qualify and some do not. [VERIFY: automatic rules and benchmark description on the DPA page]

The only reliable way to check a specific practice is to enter its street address in the Health Workforce Locator and note the date. We do exactly that for every advert on our jobs board.

## How the two interact in real life

| Your situation | 19AA | 19AB |
|---|---|---|
| Temporary resident, no fellowship, working in a DPA | Not required to hold specialist registration | Restricted; DPA location gives access |
| Temporary resident with fellowship | Satisfied by specialist registration | Still restricted, indefinitely while a temporary resident [VERIFY: "continue unchanged if you remain a temporary resident"] |
| Permanent resident with fellowship, inside ten years | Satisfied | Still restricted until ten years have passed or scaling applies |
| Permanent resident with fellowship, after ten years | Satisfied | No longer restricted [VERIFY: "end after 10 years if you are a permanent resident or citizen"] |

## What to do with this

If you are unsure which row you sit in, do not guess and do not rely on what a colleague was told. Your date of first registration, your residency status and the exact address of the practice decide it. Tell us those three things and we will tell you plainly what applies, with the source.
